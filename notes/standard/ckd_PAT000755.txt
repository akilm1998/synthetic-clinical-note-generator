Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for chronic kidney disease.

Subjective

Ethan Griffin is a 98-year-old man, height 181 cm, weight 81.6 kg, BMI 24.9 kg/m², presenting for outpatient follow-up of chronic kidney disease present for approximately 17 years. He reports ongoing fatigue. He denies pruritus, muscle cramps, nausea, peripheral edema, change in urinary frequency, sleep disturbance, or shortness of breath. He does not smoke.

His chronic comorbid conditions include anemia of chronic disease for approximately 48 years, hyperlipidemia for 43 years, and secondary hyperparathyroidism for 35 years. No current medication list was provided for review.

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 152/89 mmHg, pulse 76 bpm, respiratory rate 21/min, temperature 36.7°C, oxygen saturation 98%.

Recent laboratory results: serum creatinine 6.0 mg/dL, estimated GFR 10 mL/min/1.73 m², and BUN 60 mg/dL.

No new physical examination findings were provided. He has no reported edema or respiratory symptoms.

Assessment

1. Chronic kidney disease, longstanding, with markedly reduced renal function. Creatinine is 6.0 mg/dL, eGFR 10 mL/min/1.73 m², and BUN 60 mg/dL. He has fatigue but no current nausea, pruritus, muscle cramps, sleep disturbance, edema, urinary change, or dyspnea. Disease control requires prompt reassessment and active renal management.

2. Elevated blood pressure reading in the setting of chronic kidney disease. Blood pressure today is 152/89 mmHg.

3. Anemia of chronic disease, longstanding. Fatigue may be related, although no current blood count is available for review.

4. Secondary hyperparathyroidism, longstanding.

5. Hyperlipidemia, longstanding.

Plan

Chronic kidney disease: Recommend prompt nephrology evaluation given the substantially reduced eGFR and elevated creatinine, with review of renal replacement therapy planning and goals of care appropriate to the patient’s overall health and preferences. Obtain updated renal function and electrolyte testing, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, complete blood count, and intact parathyroid hormone as clinically appropriate. Renal ultrasound may be considered if not previously evaluated or if there is concern for structural disease. Review all prescription and over-the-counter medications for renal dosing and avoidance of nephrotoxic agents. Counsel regarding prompt evaluation for worsening shortness of breath, new swelling, persistent nausea or vomiting, confusion, marked weakness, or significant change in urination.

Blood pressure: Recheck blood pressure with appropriate outpatient monitoring and review current treatment regimen at the next medication reconciliation. Management should account for renal function and the patient’s age and treatment goals.

Anemia: Review updated complete blood count and assess for worsening fatigue or other symptoms. Further treatment should be guided by the laboratory findings and renal management plan.

Secondary hyperparathyroidism: Reassess intact parathyroid hormone and available mineral metabolism studies before adjusting therapy. Coordinate management with nephrology.

Hyperlipidemia: Review current lipid-lowering therapy and overall cardiovascular risk during medication reconciliation.

Follow-up

Close outpatient follow-up after updated laboratory evaluation and nephrology assessment, or sooner for any new uremic or fluid-overload symptoms.
