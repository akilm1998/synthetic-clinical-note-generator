Chief Complaint

Initial consultation for chronic kidney disease.

Subjective

Preston Oliver is a 54-year-old man presenting for initial outpatient evaluation of chronic kidney disease, reportedly present for approximately 22 years. He has a 19-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and a 9-year history of coronary artery disease. He does not smoke. Current weight is 66.4 kg, height 161 cm, BMI 25.6 kg/m².

He reports fatigue. He denies pruritus, muscle cramps, nausea, peripheral edema, changes in urination frequency, sleep disturbance, and shortness of breath. No current symptoms suggestive of fluid overload or overt uremic syndrome are reported. Medication use, allergies, prior renal imaging, and previous nephrology interventions are not provided.

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 143/84 mmHg, pulse 78 bpm, respiratory rate 15/min, temperature 37.0°C, oxygen saturation 97% on room air.

Laboratory data:
Serum creatinine: 5.11 mg/dL
BUN: 89 mg/dL
Estimated GFR: 10 mL/min/1.73 m²

No additional laboratory or imaging findings are available for review.

Assessment

1. Chronic kidney disease with markedly impaired renal filtration and significant azotemia. Creatinine is 5.11 mg/dL, BUN 89 mg/dL, and estimated GFR 10 mL/min/1.73 m² in the setting of approximately 22 years of known disease. The etiology is likely multifactorial given longstanding diabetes and cardiovascular disease, although the specific underlying renal pathology is not established from the available information. He has fatigue but no reported nausea, pruritus, muscle cramps, sleep disturbance, edema, dyspnea, or urinary frequency change.

2. Type 2 diabetes mellitus, longstanding, with renal disease as a major concern.

3. Coronary artery disease, present for approximately 9 years.

4. Elevated blood pressure at today’s visit. Long-term antihypertensive regimen is not provided.

Plan

Recommend prompt nephrology management given the markedly reduced estimated GFR and elevated BUN and creatinine. Discussed the need for close monitoring for progressive uremic symptoms, volume overload, electrolyte or acid-base complications, and declining functional status. He should seek urgent medical attention for new or worsening shortness of breath, chest pain, persistent nausea or vomiting, confusion, profound weakness, rapidly increasing swelling, or substantially reduced urine output.

Recommend obtaining a comprehensive renal evaluation, including serum electrolytes, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphorus, CBC, intact parathyroid hormone, urinalysis with urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and renal ultrasound. These studies would help assess complications of chronic kidney disease and clarify renal structure and proteinuria burden.

Recommend review and optimization of blood pressure and diabetes management with attention to renal and cardiovascular risk reduction. Medication reconciliation is needed before treatment recommendations are finalized; avoid nephrotoxic medications and adjust all renally cleared medications appropriately.

Given the degree of renal impairment, begin education regarding kidney replacement therapy options, including dialysis modalities and conservative kidney management, and assess timing for vascular access planning if clinically appropriate. Continue cardiovascular risk-factor management and dietary counseling focused on sodium moderation and individualized protein, potassium, and phosphorus intake after review of additional laboratory data.

Arrange close follow-up with nephrology and repeat renal function and complication monitoring promptly.
