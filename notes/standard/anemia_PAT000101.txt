Chief Complaint

Initial outpatient consultation for longstanding anemia.

History of Present Illness

Jordan Spencer is an 87-year-old man, height 188 cm, weight 76.7 kg, BMI 21.7 kg/m², presenting for evaluation of anemia reportedly present for approximately 40 years. He has a history of inflammatory bowel disease for 11 years. The anemia has not been actively managed. He denies fatigue, generalized weakness, dizziness, shortness of breath, palpitations, or reduced exercise tolerance. He reports headache and appears pale. He does not smoke.

Objective

Vital Signs: Blood pressure 135/78 mmHg, pulse 83 bpm, respiratory rate 21/min, temperature 36.9°C, oxygen saturation 98%.

General: Older man in no apparent acute distress. Pallor noted.

Cardiovascular: Heart rate regular; cardiovascular examination otherwise normal.

Respiratory: Respirations mildly increased at 21/min but oxygenation is preserved. Respiratory examination otherwise normal.

Laboratory Data:
Hemoglobin 8.2 g/dL
Hematocrit 25.3%
RBC count 2.72 million/µL
MCV 93 fL
Serum iron 39 µg/dL
Ferritin 351 ng/mL
TIBC 211 µg/dL
Transferrin saturation 18.5%

Assessment

1. Chronic normocytic anemia, most consistent with anemia of chronic inflammation associated with longstanding inflammatory bowel disease. Hemoglobin is substantially reduced at 8.2 g/dL with corresponding low hematocrit and RBC count. The normal MCV, low serum iron, elevated ferritin, low TIBC, and low-normal transferrin saturation support impaired iron availability in the setting of chronic inflammation rather than uncomplicated absolute iron deficiency. The approximately 40-year duration and absence of cardiopulmonary symptoms suggest a chronic process with current clinical stability, although the degree of anemia warrants timely evaluation and management.

2. Inflammatory bowel disease, present for approximately 11 years. Chronic intestinal inflammation may contribute to anemia through inflammatory iron sequestration, impaired erythropoiesis, nutritional deficiency, or occult gastrointestinal blood loss. Current disease activity and bleeding history should be clarified.

3. Headache and pallor, likely associated with the anemia. He is currently hemodynamically stable with normal oxygen saturation and no reported dyspnea, chest symptoms, palpitations, dizziness, or reduced exercise tolerance.

Plan

Obtain a repeat complete blood count with reticulocyte count and peripheral smear to confirm the current degree and morphology of anemia and assess marrow response.

Evaluate for other potentially reversible contributors with vitamin B12, folate, renal function testing, and inflammatory markers. Given the low serum iron and transferrin saturation, assess for concurrent iron deficiency despite the elevated ferritin, recognizing that ferritin may be increased with chronic inflammation.

Review inflammatory bowel disease activity, including gastrointestinal symptoms and any evidence of overt or occult blood loss. Coordinate management of the underlying inflammatory disease as appropriate.

Consider hematology and gastroenterology consultation if the anemia persists, worsens, or the evaluation is nondiagnostic. Iron replacement strategy, including oral versus intravenous therapy, should be guided by the additional evaluation, inflammatory bowel disease activity, and ability to absorb or tolerate oral iron. An erythropoietin-stimulating approach would require individualized consideration after assessment of renal function and other causes.

Discussed the importance of prompt evaluation for worsening fatigue, dyspnea, chest discomfort, palpitations, syncope, dizziness, overt gastrointestinal bleeding, or rapidly progressive symptoms, with urgent assessment if these occur. Arrange close outpatient follow-up to review repeat testing and establish an active treatment plan.
