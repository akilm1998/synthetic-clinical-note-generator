Chief Complaint

Medication review and follow-up of hypertension.

Subjective

Chase Fuller is an 81-year-old man with a 15-year history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus for 27 years, hyperlipidemia for 45 years, and chronic kidney disease for 27 years. He presents for review of blood pressure control and treatment tolerance. Current medication regimen is not provided; therefore, adherence and medication-related adverse effects cannot be fully assessed today.

He reports fatigue but denies headache, dizziness, blurred vision, chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, or epistaxis. He does not smoke. Weight is 83.5 kg at a height of 182 cm, with BMI 25.2 kg/m².

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 181/90 mmHg, pulse 73 bpm, respiratory rate 16/min, temperature 37.0°C, oxygen saturation 98% on room air.

Laboratory data: Sodium 140 mmol/L, potassium 4.8 mmol/L.

No additional examination findings or laboratory results are provided.

Assessment

Essential hypertension with markedly elevated office blood pressure despite absence of symptoms suggestive of acute end-organ injury. Longstanding hypertension is occurring in the setting of chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia, placing him at increased cardiovascular and renal risk. Current control is inadequate. No current evidence from the history of hypertensive emergency, although the blood pressure warrants prompt reassessment and treatment planning.

Fatigue, nonspecific.

Chronic kidney disease and diabetes mellitus are important considerations when selecting and titrating antihypertensive therapy. Potassium is upper-normal at 4.8 mmol/L, which is relevant if renin-angiotensin system blockade is initiated or intensified.

Plan

Repeat blood pressure after seated rest using an appropriately sized cuff and obtain measurements in both arms if not already done. Encourage home blood pressure monitoring with a written log, including timing relative to any prescribed medications.

Clarify the complete current medication list, including prescription, over-the-counter, and nonprescription agents, before making regimen changes. Assess adherence, access to medications, dosing schedule, and tolerance at the next medication reconciliation. Given the markedly elevated reading and lack of a documented active regimen, arrange prompt outpatient reassessment for initiation or adjustment of antihypertensive therapy rather than assuming current treatment.

Consider a gradual, individualized blood pressure reduction because of advanced age and chronic kidney disease, with close monitoring for orthostatic symptoms, dizziness, renal function changes, and potassium elevation. Review the appropriateness of standard antihypertensive options in the context of diabetes and kidney disease once the medication history and renal indices are confirmed.

Recommend dietary sodium reduction, avoidance of excess alcohol, regular activity as tolerated, and continued weight maintenance. Continue avoidance of tobacco.

Recommend monitoring of renal function and serum electrolytes after any antihypertensive initiation or dose change, particularly if an ACE inhibitor, ARB, or diuretic is used. Consider ECG, lipid profile, and urinalysis as part of cardiovascular and kidney risk assessment if not recently completed.

Seek emergency care immediately for chest pain, shortness of breath, severe headache, new neurologic symptoms, acute visual changes, confusion, or recurrent blood pressure readings in a similarly severe range with symptoms. Follow up promptly for blood pressure reassessment and medication reconciliation.
