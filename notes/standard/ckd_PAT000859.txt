Routine follow-up for chronic kidney disease.

Laboratory data: serum creatinine 6.0 mg/dL, estimated GFR 7 mL/min/1.73 m², BUN 76 mg/dL.

Assessment

Chronic kidney disease with markedly reduced renal function and significant azotemia. The creatinine is 6.0 mg/dL, eGFR 7 mL/min/1.73 m², and BUN 76 mg/dL in the setting of longstanding disease. Fatigue may be related to advanced renal dysfunction, although anemia and other metabolic complications require evaluation. He currently denies nausea, pruritus, cramps, sleep disturbance, oliguria-type urinary changes, edema, or dyspnea. Blood pressure is mildly elevated. Given the degree of renal impairment and limited current disease management, this requires expedited renal evaluation and assessment for complications and renal replacement therapy planning.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus, longstanding, with chronic kidney disease. Glycemic data are not available for review today.

Hyperlipidemia, longstanding.

Coronary artery disease, diagnosed approximately 4 years ago. No anginal or heart failure symptoms are reported today.

Plan

Arrange expedited nephrology evaluation for advanced chronic kidney disease, including assessment of renal replacement therapy options and preparation as clinically indicated. The current renal indices warrant prompt review rather than routine surveillance alone.

Obtain serum electrolytes, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphorus, complete blood count, intact parathyroid hormone, and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio to assess for metabolic, hematologic, mineral-bone, and proteinuric complications. Consider renal ultrasound if not previously performed or if structural assessment is needed.

Review and reconcile all prescription, over-the-counter, and herbal medications at the next contact, with renal-dose adjustment and avoidance of nephrotoxic agents as appropriate. No specific medication regimen is documented today, so no medication changes are made in this note.

Continue monitoring blood pressure and diabetes control, with individualized targets given his age, coronary artery disease, and severe renal impairment. Coordinate management of cardiovascular risk factors with his primary care and cardiology teams as appropriate.

Discuss urgent evaluation for new or worsening shortness of breath, chest pain, confusion, persistent nausea or vomiting, marked weakness, new edema, or significant change in urine output, as these may indicate complications requiring immediate assessment.

Return promptly after renal evaluation and review of the requested laboratory studies.
