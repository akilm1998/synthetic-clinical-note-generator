Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Subjective

Janet McKenzie is a 32-year-old woman presenting for routine outpatient follow-up of type 2 diabetes, diagnosed approximately 12 years ago. She is not currently following an active diabetes management plan. She denies increased thirst, frequent urination, increased hunger, unintentional weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, recurrent infections, numbness, or tingling of the hands or feet.

Recent laboratory testing shows HbA1c 9.9% and fasting glucose 231 mg/dL, consistent with inadequate glycemic control. She has a history of hypertension for approximately 1 year and dyslipidemia for approximately 2 years. She does not smoke.

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 139/86 mmHg, pulse 71 bpm, respiratory rate 16/min, temperature 36.9°C, oxygen saturation 99%.

Height 159 cm, weight 58.7 kg, BMI 23.2 kg/m².

Laboratory results: HbA1c 9.9%. Fasting glucose 231 mg/dL.

No acute hyperglycemic or infectious symptoms reported.

Assessment

Type 2 diabetes mellitus without reported complications, currently poorly controlled based on HbA1c of 9.9% and fasting glucose of 231 mg/dL. Despite the absence of symptomatic hyperglycemia, the degree of elevation warrants prompt treatment intensification and evaluation for end-organ involvement.

Hypertension, approximately 1 year in duration, with mildly elevated office blood pressure today.

Dyslipidemia, approximately 2 years in duration.

Plan

Discussed the significance of the elevated HbA1c and fasting glucose and the importance of establishing an active diabetes treatment plan. Reviewed nutrition, portion control, reduction of concentrated carbohydrates and sugar-sweetened beverages, regular physical activity, and continued avoidance of tobacco. Her BMI is 23.2 kg/m², so counseling will focus on glycemic quality and activity rather than weight loss.

Recommend prompt initiation or adjustment of glucose-lowering therapy after review of renal function, contraindications, and patient preferences. Metformin would generally be considered if appropriate; given the degree of hyperglycemia, escalation beyond a single agent may be necessary depending on response and repeat assessment. Review home glucose monitoring technique and establish individualized fasting and postprandial targets.

Recommend obtaining renal function and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, along with a fasting lipid profile, to assess diabetes-associated kidney and cardiovascular risk and guide treatment. Recommend comprehensive diabetes preventive care, including a dilated retinal examination and complete foot examination with assessment of sensation and pulses.

Blood pressure and dyslipidemia should be reassessed as part of overall cardiovascular risk reduction, with treatment decisions based on repeat measurements and laboratory evaluation.

Arrange close follow-up after treatment initiation and repeat HbA1c in approximately 3 months, sooner for symptoms of marked hyperglycemia such as polyuria, polydipsia, vomiting, confusion, or significant weakness.
