Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for chronic anemia.

Subjective

Sean Palmer is a 52-year-old man presenting for routine follow-up of anemia present for approximately 13 years. He reports no current fatigue, weakness, dizziness, headache, palpitations, shortness of breath, reduced exercise tolerance, or other exercise-related limitation. No pallor has been noted.

His history is significant for inflammatory bowel disease and rheumatoid arthritis, both present for approximately 12 years. He currently smokes. No anemia-directed treatment is documented.

Objective

Vital Signs: Blood pressure 126/82 mmHg, pulse 87 bpm, respiratory rate 22/min, temperature 36.9°C, oxygen saturation 96%.

Height 173 cm, weight 79.6 kg, BMI 26.6 kg/m².

Laboratory Results:
Hemoglobin 9.2 g/dL
Hematocrit 28.4%
RBC count 3.38 million/µL
MCV 84 fL
Serum iron 25 µg/dL
Ferritin 411 ng/mL
TIBC 237 µg/dL
Transferrin saturation 10.5%

Assessment

1. Chronic normocytic anemia, currently without reported symptoms. Hemoglobin and hematocrit remain reduced, with low serum iron and transferrin saturation, elevated ferritin, and low TIBC. This pattern is most consistent with anemia associated with chronic inflammation, in the setting of longstanding inflammatory bowel disease and rheumatoid arthritis, with possible functional iron restriction. There is no current evidence from the history of symptomatic cardiopulmonary compromise.

2. Inflammatory bowel disease, longstanding. Chronic inflammatory activity may be contributing to the anemia. Current disease activity and gastrointestinal blood loss status should be reviewed.

3. Rheumatoid arthritis, longstanding. Ongoing systemic inflammation may also be contributing to impaired iron utilization and reduced erythropoiesis.

4. Tobacco use. Smoking cessation remains advisable as part of overall health maintenance.

Plan

Obtain updated CBC with reticulocyte count and peripheral blood smear to further characterize the anemia and assess marrow response. Check vitamin B12, folate, renal function, and inflammatory markers as clinically indicated. Reassess iron studies in the context of inflammatory disease rather than interpreting ferritin in isolation.

Review current control of inflammatory bowel disease and rheumatoid arthritis, including any gastrointestinal symptoms, evidence of blood loss, and inflammatory flares. Coordinate management of the underlying inflammatory conditions, as improved control may help improve the anemia.

Discuss iron replacement based on the overall evaluation and gastrointestinal tolerance; intravenous iron may be considered if oral replacement is ineffective or poorly tolerated. Consider specialist evaluation if anemia persists, worsens, becomes symptomatic, or remains unexplained after the initial evaluation.

He was advised to seek prompt care for new or worsening shortness of breath, chest discomfort, palpitations, syncope, significant dizziness, bleeding, or marked decline in exercise tolerance. Follow up after repeat laboratory evaluation to update the treatment plan.
