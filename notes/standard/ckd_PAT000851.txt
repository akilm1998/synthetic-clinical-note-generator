Chief Complaint
Routine follow-up for chronic kidney disease.

Subjective
Evan Bryant is a 63-year-old man presenting for routine follow-up of chronic kidney disease diagnosed approximately 19 years ago. He reports ongoing fatigue but denies pruritus, muscle cramps, nausea, sleep disturbance, peripheral edema, change in urination frequency, or shortness of breath. He does not smoke. Weight is 78.2 kg at 179 cm (BMI 24.4 kg/m²).

Relevant comorbidities include secondary hyperparathyroidism for approximately 10 years and gout for approximately 23 years. No current medication regimen was provided.

Objective
Vital signs: BP 140/88 mmHg, pulse 74 bpm, respiratory rate 17/min, temperature 36.7°C, oxygen saturation 97% on room air.

Laboratory data:
Serum creatinine 3.1 mg/dL
BUN 63 mg/dL
Estimated GFR 19 mL/min/1.73 m²

No edema, respiratory symptoms, or other interval findings were reported.

Assessment
1. Chronic kidney disease of 19 years’ duration with significantly reduced renal function, reflected by creatinine 3.1 mg/dL, BUN 63 mg/dL, and eGFR 19 mL/min/1.73 m². The patient has fatigue but no reported uremic gastrointestinal symptoms, pruritus, fluid overload symptoms, or change in urination. Disease appears inadequately addressed at present and requires structured renal monitoring and complication assessment.

2. Hypertension, with blood pressure mildly above goal at 140/88 mmHg. Blood pressure control is important to limit further renal injury.

3. Secondary hyperparathyroidism, longstanding, in the setting of chronic kidney disease.

4. Gout, longstanding. Renal impairment is clinically relevant to urate management and medication selection.

Plan
Discussed the importance of consistent nephrology and primary care follow-up for advanced chronic kidney disease and prevention of further progression. Recommend renal-focused laboratory monitoring, including serum electrolytes, CBC, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and intact parathyroid hormone. Consider renal ultrasound as part of structural assessment if not previously completed.

Review and optimize blood pressure management, with attention to renal-protective therapy as clinically appropriate after medication reconciliation and review of potassium and renal trends. Counsel on avoidance of NSAIDs and other potentially nephrotoxic agents, and advise the patient to report reduced urine output, new swelling, worsening shortness of breath, persistent nausea or vomiting, confusion, or marked worsening of fatigue.

Continue evaluation and management of secondary hyperparathyroidism and gout in the context of reduced kidney function. Renal replacement planning and education should be addressed in ongoing nephrology care given the degree of renal impairment, while monitoring for anemia, electrolyte abnormalities, mineral-bone complications, and progression of uremic symptoms.

Follow up after repeat renal and complication-focused testing, or sooner for any concerning change in symptoms.
