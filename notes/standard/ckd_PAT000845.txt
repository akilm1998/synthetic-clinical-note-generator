Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for chronic kidney disease.

Subjective

Bob Hill is a 52-year-old man with a 15-year history of chronic kidney disease presenting for routine outpatient follow-up. He reports no current fatigue, pruritus, muscle cramps, nausea, sleep disturbance, peripheral edema, change in urinary frequency, or shortness of breath. His chronic kidney disease has not been actively managed recently. Relevant comorbidities include hyperlipidemia for approximately 12 years and secondary hyperparathyroidism diagnosed approximately 1 year ago. He continues to smoke. Current weight is 75.6 kg at a height of 162 cm, with BMI 28.8 kg/m².

Objective

Vital signs: blood pressure 132/83 mmHg, pulse 78 beats/min, respiratory rate 14/min, temperature 36.8°C, oxygen saturation 96% on room air.

Laboratory data show serum creatinine 4.62 mg/dL, BUN 93 mg/dL, and estimated GFR 11 mL/min/1.73 m².

Assessment

Chronic kidney disease of 15 years’ duration with markedly reduced renal function, reflected by creatinine 4.62 mg/dL, BUN 93 mg/dL, and eGFR 11 mL/min/1.73 m². Although he is currently without reported uremic or volume-overload symptoms and has a stable blood pressure and normal oxygen saturation, the degree of renal impairment warrants prompt reassessment and active nephrologic management. There is no clinical history today suggesting an acute symptomatic decompensation, but the laboratory values indicate advanced chronic renal dysfunction.

Secondary hyperparathyroidism, present for approximately 1 year, in the setting of chronic kidney disease.

Hyperlipidemia, longstanding, with a reported duration of 12 years.

Tobacco use.

Overweight by BMI, 28.8 kg/m².

Plan

Arrange prompt nephrology evaluation for comprehensive management of advanced chronic kidney disease and discussion of renal replacement planning as clinically appropriate. Repeat renal function assessment and obtain a serum electrolyte panel, complete blood count, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and intact parathyroid hormone level to characterize current complications and guide treatment. Renal ultrasound may be considered if not previously performed or if the underlying renal anatomy and etiology remain insufficiently characterized.

Review dietary sodium, protein, potassium, and phosphorus intake in the context of updated laboratory results, with renal nutrition counseling as appropriate. Avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and other potentially nephrotoxic exposures, and review all prescription and over-the-counter medications for renal dosing.

Continue close monitoring of blood pressure, urine output, edema, dyspnea, nausea, pruritus, muscle cramps, sleep disturbance, and progressive fatigue. He should seek urgent medical attention for new or worsening shortness of breath, marked edema, persistent vomiting, confusion, chest pain, or substantially decreased urine output.

Address tobacco cessation and weight management. Hyperlipidemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism should be incorporated into the broader renal and cardiovascular risk-reduction plan after medication and laboratory review. Follow up promptly after nephrology assessment and updated testing.
