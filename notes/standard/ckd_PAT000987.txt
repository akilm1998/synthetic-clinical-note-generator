Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for chronic kidney disease.

Subjective

Justin Richardson is a 75-year-old man presenting for outpatient follow-up of chronic kidney disease, reported for approximately 3 years. He has a 28-year history of hypertension and a 20-year history of gout. He denies fatigue, pruritus, muscle cramps, nausea, sleep disturbance, peripheral edema, changes in urination, and shortness of breath. He does not smoke. Height is 182 cm, weight 78.5 kg, BMI 23.7 kg/m².

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 171/101 mmHg, pulse 73 bpm, respiratory rate 16/min, temperature 36.8°C, oxygen saturation 97%.

Laboratory results: Serum creatinine 5.42 mg/dL, BUN 66 mg/dL, estimated GFR 9 mL/min/1.73 m².

Assessment

1. Chronic kidney disease with markedly reduced renal function. Current creatinine, BUN, and estimated GFR reflect advanced renal impairment. Despite the absence of reported uremic or fluid-overload symptoms, the degree of renal dysfunction requires prompt specialist assessment and close monitoring. No dialysis-related symptoms are reported today, but renal replacement therapy planning and assessment for indications should not be delayed.

2. Hypertension, longstanding and currently severely uncontrolled with blood pressure of 171/101 mmHg. This is a major risk factor for progression of renal disease and requires urgent reassessment and treatment planning.

3. Gout, longstanding, with a 20-year history. Advanced renal impairment substantially affects urate handling and medication selection.

Plan

Arrange urgent nephrology evaluation for comprehensive management of advanced chronic kidney disease, assessment for complications, and discussion of renal replacement therapy planning. He should seek emergency care promptly for new or worsening shortness of breath, chest pain, confusion, persistent vomiting, marked weakness, rapidly progressive swelling, or significant reduction in urine output.

Repeat renal function and obtain a serum electrolyte panel, complete blood count, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, intact parathyroid hormone, and renal ultrasound as clinically appropriate. Evaluate for anemia, mineral and bone disorder, proteinuria, and structural renal disease.

Reassess blood pressure promptly with repeat measurements and medication reconciliation. Antihypertensive therapy should be initiated or adjusted by the treating clinician with attention to renal function, potassium status, and volume status. Avoid NSAIDs and other potentially nephrotoxic agents; all prescriptions and over-the-counter medications should be reviewed for renal dosing.

Continue close outpatient follow-up while awaiting nephrology assessment.
