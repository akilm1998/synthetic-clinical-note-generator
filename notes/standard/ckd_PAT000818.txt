Chief Complaint

Initial outpatient consultation for chronic kidney disease.

Subjective

Pamela Donovan is a 50-year-old woman, 160 cm tall and 73.0 kg, with BMI 28.5 kg/m², presenting for initial evaluation of chronic kidney disease reportedly present for approximately 13 years. Current renal indices are markedly abnormal. She reports fatigue. She denies pruritus, muscle cramps, nausea, peripheral edema, increased or decreased urinary frequency, sleep disturbance, and shortness of breath.

Relevant comorbidities include type 2 diabetes mellitus for approximately 1 year, coronary artery disease for 4 years, and gout for 7 years. She does not smoke. Current medication regimen, allergies, prior renal workup, family history, and detailed dietary history were not provided for this consultation.

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 145/82 mmHg, pulse 80/min, respiratory rate 14/min, temperature 37.0°C, oxygen saturation 98% on room air.

Height 160 cm; weight 73.0 kg; BMI 28.5 kg/m².

Laboratory data: Serum creatinine 3.27 mg/dL, estimated glomerular filtration rate 16 mL/min/1.73 m², and BUN 94 mg/dL.

No additional physical examination findings or laboratory results are documented.

Assessment

Chronic kidney disease with substantially reduced renal function and elevated BUN and creatinine, present for approximately 13 years. The degree of renal impairment is significant and warrants structured nephrology management and evaluation for complications. Fatigue may be related to advanced renal dysfunction, although anemia and other metabolic contributors require assessment. She currently denies symptoms suggestive of clinically evident fluid overload or significant uremic symptom burden, including dyspnea, edema, nausea, pruritus, muscle cramps, or sleep disturbance.

Blood pressure is above goal at 145/82 mmHg in the setting of chronic kidney disease and coronary artery disease.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus of 1 year’s duration, a potential contributor to ongoing kidney disease and an important modifiable risk factor.

Coronary artery disease, duration 4 years.

Gout, duration 7 years; renal function should be considered carefully when selecting urate-lowering or flare therapies.

Overweight by BMI.

Plan

Recommend prompt establishment or continuation of nephrology care given eGFR 16 mL/min/1.73 m², BUN 94 mg/dL, long disease duration, and associated fatigue. Discuss renal replacement therapy education and future planning as clinically appropriate, while recognizing that treatment decisions should be based on symptoms, metabolic complications, trajectory, and overall clinical assessment rather than a single laboratory value.

Obtain repeat renal function testing with serum electrolytes, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphorus, and albumin, together with complete blood count to evaluate for anemia or other contributors to fatigue. Check urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio for proteinuria assessment and risk stratification. Recommend intact parathyroid hormone measurement to evaluate for CKD-related mineral and bone disorder. Renal ultrasound is appropriate if not previously completed to assess kidney size and structural disease.

Perform complete medication reconciliation before initiating or adjusting therapy. Avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and other potentially nephrotoxic agents. Any gout treatment should be selected and dosed for markedly reduced kidney function. Review diabetes therapies for renal appropriateness and reinforce individualized glycemic control without hypoglycemia.

Monitor blood pressure closely, with individualized target-setting given concurrent coronary artery disease. Dietary counseling should emphasize sodium restriction, avoidance of excessive processed foods, and individualized protein, potassium, phosphorus, and fluid guidance based on follow-up laboratory results and nephrology recommendations. Encourage weight management and continued smoking avoidance.

Educate regarding symptoms requiring urgent evaluation, including new or worsening shortness of breath, rapidly progressive swelling, persistent vomiting, confusion, marked weakness, chest pain, or substantially reduced urine output.

Arrange close outpatient follow-up after repeat laboratory evaluation, with ongoing monitoring of renal function, blood pressure, volume status, fatigue, and development of uremic or mineral-bone complications.
