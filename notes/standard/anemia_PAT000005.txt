Chief Complaint

Initial consultation for longstanding anemia.

History of Present Illness

Grant Porter is a 96-year-old man, height 190 cm, weight 106.9 kg, BMI 29.6 kg/m², presenting for evaluation of anemia present for approximately 57 years. He currently denies fatigue, weakness, dizziness, headache, shortness of breath, palpitations, reduced exercise tolerance, or subjective pallor. He is a lifelong nonsmoker by history.

Relevant chronic conditions include chronic kidney disease for approximately 37 years, inflammatory bowel disease for approximately 75 years, and chronic infection for approximately 23 years. These conditions provide significant potential contributors to chronic inflammation and impaired erythropoiesis. There is no reported current treatment plan for the anemia in the available history.

Review of Systems

Constitutional: Denies fatigue, weakness, or reduced exercise tolerance.
Cardiopulmonary: Denies dyspnea, palpitations, or dizziness.
Neurologic: Denies headache.
Hematologic: Denies subjective pallor.

Vital Signs

Blood pressure 148/77 mmHg, pulse 80 bpm, respiratory rate 20/min, temperature 36.7°C, oxygen saturation 98%.

Physical Examination

Patient is documented as afebrile with normal oxygen saturation and a normal resting pulse. No pallor is reported. Cardiovascular and respiratory findings are not suggestive of acute decompensation based on the available vital signs and symptom review.

Laboratory Data

Hemoglobin 8.5 g/dL, hematocrit 25.2%, RBC count 2.86 million/µL, MCV 88 fL. Serum iron 48 µg/dL, ferritin 371 ng/mL, TIBC 248 µg/dL, and transferrin saturation 19.4%.

Assessment

Longstanding normocytic anemia, most consistent with anemia of chronic disease/chronic inflammation in the setting of chronic kidney disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and chronic infection. The low hemoglobin, hematocrit, and RBC count represent clinically significant anemia despite the absence of current symptoms. Normal MCV, elevated ferritin, reduced TIBC, and low-normal transferrin saturation support inflammatory iron restriction rather than uncomplicated absolute iron deficiency, although mixed etiologies remain possible. Chronic kidney disease may also contribute through impaired erythropoietin production. There is no evidence from the current history or vital signs of symptomatic cardiopulmonary compromise.

Plan

Obtain a complete blood count with reticulocyte count and peripheral smear to further characterize marrow response and red-cell morphology. Check vitamin B12 and folate levels, renal function, and inflammatory markers as clinically appropriate. Reassess iron availability in the context of chronic inflammation and inflammatory bowel disease; avoid assuming that the elevated ferritin excludes functional iron restriction.

Review the activity and treatment status of the chronic kidney disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and chronic infection, as control of underlying disease is central to management. Evaluate for occult blood loss or gastrointestinal blood loss if clinically indicated, particularly given the history of inflammatory bowel disease.

Consider coordinated management with nephrology and hematology regarding persistent hemoglobin of 8.5 g/dL and possible use of erythropoiesis-directed therapy or iron replacement after the above evaluation. Any oral or intravenous iron therapy should be individualized based on renal function, inflammatory activity, iron indices, and gastrointestinal tolerance.

Because of the degree of anemia and advanced age, advise prompt evaluation for new dyspnea, chest discomfort, syncope, significant dizziness, palpitations, active bleeding, or rapidly worsening weakness. Arrange close outpatient follow-up after repeat laboratory assessment.
