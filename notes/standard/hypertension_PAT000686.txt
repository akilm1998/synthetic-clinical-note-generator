Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for longstanding hypertension.

Subjective

Michael Brooks is a 70-year-old man, nonsmoker, presenting for routine follow-up of essential hypertension diagnosed approximately 25 years ago. Blood pressure control has not been actively managed. He reports intermittent dizziness, palpitations, and fatigue. He denies headache, blurred vision, chest pain, shortness of breath, or epistaxis. There is no reported symptom complex suggestive of acute hypertensive emergency.

Relevant comorbidities include type 2 diabetes mellitus for 38 years and coronary artery disease for 10 years. He has no reported tobacco use. Current medication use is not provided.

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 162/91 mmHg; pulse 75 bpm; respiratory rate 16/min; temperature 36.7°C; oxygen saturation 98% on room air.

Height 186 cm; weight 73.7 kg; BMI 21.3 kg/m².

Serum electrolytes: sodium 135 mmol/L; potassium 4.5 mmol/L.

No additional examination findings are documented today.

Assessment

Essential hypertension with persistently elevated office blood pressure and suboptimal control in the setting of longstanding disease. His dizziness, palpitations, and fatigue may be related to uncontrolled blood pressure but are not specific. There is no current chest pain, dyspnea, neurologic visual symptom, or other reported feature of hypertensive crisis. His age, diabetes, and established coronary artery disease place him at substantially increased cardiovascular risk and support prompt reassessment and active management.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus, longstanding, reported duration 38 years.

Coronary artery disease, reported duration 10 years.

Mild borderline hyponatremia with sodium 135 mmol/L; potassium is within the provided reference context at 4.5 mmol/L. Renal function cannot be assessed from the available laboratory data.

Plan

Repeat blood pressure measurement with appropriate cuff and confirm control using serial home readings or ambulatory monitoring if feasible. Review adherence, access to care, dietary sodium intake, alcohol use, physical activity, and any prior antihypertensive treatment before selecting or initiating therapy. Given the degree and duration of elevation and his diabetes and coronary artery disease, pharmacologic treatment should be addressed promptly rather than managed with lifestyle measures alone.

Recommend a low-sodium, heart-healthy diet, regular physical activity as tolerated, and continued avoidance of tobacco. Maintain a written blood pressure and symptom log.

Obtain an ECG to evaluate palpitations and assess for hypertensive or ischemic cardiac effects. Recommend lipid profile and urinalysis as part of cardiovascular and end-organ risk assessment; additional renal function testing is clinically appropriate before and during antihypertensive therapy.

Continue coordinated management of diabetes and coronary artery disease. Seek urgent evaluation for chest pain, new shortness of breath, focal neurologic symptoms, severe headache, acute visual change, or markedly higher blood pressure with symptoms.

Arrange close outpatient reassessment after blood pressure monitoring and medication reconciliation to update the treatment plan.
