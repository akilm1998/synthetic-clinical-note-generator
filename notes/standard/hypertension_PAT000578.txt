Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for hypertension.

Subjective

Richard Lawson is a 42-year-old man presenting for routine outpatient follow-up of hypertension present for approximately 9 years. Blood pressure control has not been actively managed. He denies headache, dizziness, blurred vision, chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, fatigue, and epistaxis. No acute cardiopulmonary or neurologic symptoms are reported.

His medical history is also significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus for 6 years and chronic kidney disease for 2 years. He does not smoke. Current weight is 65.2 kg at 168 cm (BMI 23.1 kg/m²). No medication regimen is provided for review today.

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 162/98 mmHg, pulse 72 bpm, respiratory rate 18/min, temperature 36.6°C, oxygen saturation 97%.

Laboratory data: Sodium 135 mmol/L, potassium 4.6 mmol/L.

No additional examination findings are documented today.

Assessment

1. Essential hypertension, longstanding and currently inadequately controlled. Blood pressure is significantly elevated at 162/98 mmHg despite absence of symptoms suggestive of acute end-organ injury. The combination of hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease places him at increased cardiovascular and renal risk. Current potassium is 4.6 mmol/L and sodium is 135 mmol/L.

2. Type 2 diabetes mellitus, present for 6 years. Diabetes increases his risk for hypertensive and renal complications; current glycemic control is not assessed in the available information.

3. Chronic kidney disease, present for 2 years. Hypertension and diabetes are important potential contributors to progression, and blood pressure control is a priority.

Plan

Hypertension: Confirm the elevated reading with repeat office measurement and encourage accurate home blood pressure monitoring with a written log. Discussed the importance of consistent medical management, dietary sodium reduction, regular physical activity as tolerated, and avoidance of excess alcohol. Given persistent elevation in the setting of diabetes and chronic kidney disease, prompt clinician-directed initiation or adjustment of antihypertensive therapy is recommended, with consideration of a kidney-protective renin-angiotensin system agent when clinically appropriate. Monitor renal function and serum electrolytes after treatment changes. Obtain an ECG, lipid profile, and urinalysis as part of cardiovascular and renal risk assessment.

Diabetes mellitus: Continue coordinated management with attention to blood pressure and renal protection. Review current diabetes therapy and glycemic monitoring at the next medication reconciliation.

Chronic kidney disease: Avoid nephrotoxic medications when possible and monitor renal function and electrolytes during antihypertensive management. Urinalysis is recommended to assess for evidence of renal involvement.

Safety: Although he is asymptomatic today, he was advised to seek urgent evaluation for severe headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, new neurologic symptoms, visual changes, or epistaxis. Arrange close outpatient follow-up for blood pressure reassessment and treatment planning.
