Chief Complaint

Initial outpatient consultation for evaluation and management of chronic anemia.

History of Present Illness

Matthew Reed is a 91-year-old man, 185 cm tall and weighing 81.1 kg with BMI 23.7 kg/m², referred for evaluation of longstanding anemia. The anemia has been present for approximately 61 years and has not been under active management. He currently denies fatigue, generalized weakness, dizziness, headache, pallor, reduced exercise tolerance, shortness of breath, palpitations, or other anemia-related symptoms.

Available laboratory testing demonstrates hemoglobin 8.7 g/dL, hematocrit 25.8%, RBC count 2.87 million/µL, and MCV 90 fL. Serum iron is reduced at 39 µg/dL, with ferritin 321 ng/mL, TIBC 210 µg/dL, and transferrin saturation 18.6%. The normocytic indices, reduced serum iron and TIBC, and elevated ferritin are most consistent with anemia associated with chronic inflammation and/or chronic disease, although the degree of anemia warrants confirmation and evaluation for additional contributing factors.

Relevant chronic conditions include rheumatoid arthritis of 18 years’ duration, heart failure of 14 years’ duration, and malignancy of 21 years’ duration. He is a current smoker. No medication regimen is provided.

Review of Systems

Constitutional: Denies fatigue, weakness, or reduced exercise tolerance.

Cardiovascular: Denies palpitations.

Respiratory: Denies shortness of breath. Respiratory rate is elevated at 25/min despite normal oxygen saturation.

Neurologic: Denies dizziness or headache.

Hematologic: No pallor reported.

The available history is otherwise negative for the anemia-related symptoms listed above.

Objective

Vital Signs: Blood pressure 136/78 mmHg, pulse 90 bpm, respiratory rate 25/min, temperature 36.7°C, oxygen saturation 97% on room air.

General: Elderly male with normal body mass index based on documented height and weight. No anemia-related symptoms are reported.

Cardiovascular: Pulse is borderline elevated at 90 bpm. No cardiovascular abnormality is provided in the available examination information.

Respiratory: Tachypnea is present by measured respiratory rate. Oxygen saturation is preserved at 97%. No respiratory abnormality is provided in the available examination information.

Laboratory Data: Hemoglobin 8.7 g/dL; hematocrit 25.8%; RBC 2.87 million/µL; MCV 90 fL; serum iron 39 µg/dL; ferritin 321 ng/mL; TIBC 210 µg/dL; transferrin saturation 18.6%.

Assessment

Anemia consistent with chronic disease and chronic inflammation. The anemia is longstanding and currently untreated. It is significant in severity by hemoglobin concentration but clinically compensated by the absence of reported fatigue, dyspnea, dizziness, palpitations, or reduced exercise tolerance. The normocytic pattern with low serum iron, low TIBC, and elevated ferritin supports impaired iron availability related to inflammation rather than uncomplicated absolute iron deficiency. His long-standing rheumatoid arthritis, heart failure, and malignancy are all potential contributors through inflammatory cytokine activity, altered iron sequestration, reduced erythropoiesis, and possible chronic organ dysfunction.

The transferrin saturation of 18.6% indicates limited circulating iron availability. Ferritin is not low, making isolated iron deficiency less likely, but concurrent absolute iron deficiency cannot be excluded in an elderly patient without further evaluation. The chronicity and hemoglobin level also warrant assessment for other reversible causes, including nutritional deficiency, renal contribution, occult blood loss, marrow disease, or progression of the underlying malignancy. These are considerations for evaluation and are not established diagnoses from the available information.

Rheumatoid arthritis, longstanding. Chronic inflammatory activity may be contributing substantially to the anemia.

Heart failure, longstanding. Heart failure-related inflammation and possible renal dysfunction may contribute to impaired erythropoiesis. The increased respiratory rate should be reassessed clinically even though the patient denies shortness of breath and has normal oxygen saturation.

Malignancy, longstanding. The underlying malignancy may contribute to inflammatory anemia or marrow suppression; the type and current activity are not specified in the available information.

Current tobacco use. Smoking is an additional cardiopulmonary risk factor and may complicate interpretation of exertional symptoms, although none are currently reported.

Plan

Confirm the hematologic pattern with a repeat complete blood count including red-cell indices and platelet count, reticulocyte count, and peripheral blood smear. Obtain vitamin B12, folate, and renal function testing, together with inflammatory markers, as clinically appropriate. Review prior blood counts to establish the trajectory and determine whether the current hemoglobin represents a change from his long-term baseline.

Reassess iron availability in clinical context. Given the elevated ferritin and low TIBC, empiric oral iron should not be assumed to be beneficial without clarification of absolute versus functional iron deficiency. Intravenous iron may be considered only if subsequent evaluation supports iron-restricted erythropoiesis and the expected benefit outweighs treatment risks.

Management should focus on identifying and controlling active inflammatory disease, reviewing the status of the longstanding malignancy, and assessing for renal contribution. An erythropoiesis-stimulating agent would require careful specialist consideration because of advanced age, heart failure, malignancy, and the associated thrombotic and cardiovascular risks; no such therapy is initiated based on the information currently available.

Assess for occult blood loss and other sources of chronic blood loss as clinically appropriate, particularly if iron deficiency is demonstrated or the anemia worsens. Hematology involvement is appropriate given the hemoglobin of 8.7 g/dL, the prolonged duration, advanced age, multiple potential contributors, and absence of prior active management.

Reassess the elevated respiratory rate and cardiac status during the clinical examination. He should seek urgent evaluation for new or worsening shortness of breath, chest pain, syncope, sustained palpitations, marked weakness, overt bleeding, confusion, or other evidence of cardiopulmonary compromise.

Follow-up should occur after the recommended evaluation to review the blood count, reticulocyte response, nutritional studies, renal function, inflammatory profile, and clinical status.
