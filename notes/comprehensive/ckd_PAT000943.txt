Chief Complaint

Initial outpatient consultation for chronic kidney disease and markedly reduced kidney function.

Subjective

Victoria Richardson is an 87-year-old woman, 164 cm tall and weighing 77.2 kg, with BMI 28.7 kg/m², presenting for initial evaluation of chronic kidney disease. She reports fatigue. She denies pruritus, nausea, muscle cramps, sleep disturbance, peripheral edema, change in urinary frequency, and shortness of breath. No current symptoms of overt fluid overload or a prominent uremic prodrome are reported.

She has had chronic kidney disease for approximately 49 years. Her major renal risk factors include type 2 diabetes mellitus of 47 years’ duration and longstanding cardiovascular disease. Additional medical history includes hyperlipidemia for 5 years and coronary artery disease for 8 years. She currently smokes. Medication use, allergies, prior renal imaging, dialysis history, and family history are not established in the information provided and should be reviewed during medication and history reconciliation.

Respiratory: Denies shortness of breath.

Gastrointestinal: Denies nausea.

Genitourinary: Denies change in urinary frequency.

Neurologic and sleep: Denies muscle cramps and sleep disturbance.

Objective

Vital signs: Blood pressure 151/86 mmHg, pulse 77 bpm, respiratory rate 17/min, temperature 36.7°C, oxygen saturation 95%.

Anthropometrics: Height 164 cm, weight 77.2 kg, BMI 28.7 kg/m².

Laboratory data: Serum creatinine 4.94 mg/dL. Estimated glomerular filtration rate 9 mL/min/1.73 m². Blood urea nitrogen 78 mg/dL.

Physical examination: Available objective findings are notable for elevated blood pressure. The patient reports no peripheral edema or shortness of breath. No additional examination findings are provided.

Assessment

Chronic kidney disease with markedly reduced renal filtration. Creatinine is 4.94 mg/dL, eGFR 9 mL/min/1.73 m², and BUN 78 mg/dL, consistent with advanced chronic renal impairment. The long duration of disease, 47-year history of diabetes, hypertension-range blood pressure, and coronary artery disease make diabetic and hypertensive kidney injury clinically plausible, although the underlying etiology requires confirmation. She has fatigue but currently denies nausea, pruritus, muscle cramps, sleep disturbance, altered urinary frequency, edema, or dyspnea. There is no reported evidence of symptomatic fluid overload at this visit. The degree of renal dysfunction warrants prompt specialist assessment and planning for renal replacement therapy or conservative kidney management in accordance with her goals and overall health.

Hypertension, with today’s blood pressure 151/86 mmHg, requiring confirmation and treatment review in the context of severe kidney disease and coronary artery disease.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus, longstanding for 47 years, a major risk factor for progressive kidney disease. Current glycemic control and treatment regimen are not documented.

Coronary artery disease, present for 8 years, increasing her cardiovascular risk in the setting of severe chronic kidney disease.

Hyperlipidemia, present for 5 years.

Tobacco use, ongoing, contributing to cardiovascular and renal risk.

Fatigue, likely multifactorial and potentially related to advanced kidney disease; anemia and other metabolic contributors should be evaluated.

Plan

Recommend expedited nephrology referral for comprehensive evaluation of severe chronic kidney dysfunction, discussion of prognosis, assessment for reversible contributors, and individualized planning regarding dialysis, conservative kidney management, and goals of care. The decision regarding renal replacement therapy should incorporate symptoms, metabolic complications, functional status, comorbid coronary artery disease, age, and the patient’s preferences rather than eGFR alone.

Obtain recommended renal evaluation including repeat serum creatinine and eGFR, serum electrolytes, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphorus, complete blood count, iron studies as clinically appropriate, and intact parathyroid hormone. Obtain urinalysis and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio to characterize renal injury. Renal ultrasound is appropriate to assess kidney size and structural or obstructive disease. These studies should be arranged promptly through primary care or nephrology.

Review all prescription, over-the-counter, and herbal medications before treatment decisions. Avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and other potentially nephrotoxic agents. Medication dosing should be adjusted for the current degree of renal impairment, and iodinated contrast should be avoided unless essential with appropriate renal planning.

Reassess blood pressure with home readings or repeat office measurements. Develop an antihypertensive plan after medication reconciliation, with attention to renal function, potassium, volume status, and coronary artery disease. Do not initiate or adjust renally active medications without current electrolyte data and review of her complete medication list.

Review diabetes management and glycemic targets in the context of advanced age, severe renal impairment, cardiovascular disease, and risk of hypoglycemia. Coordinate diabetes care with nephrology and primary care.

Evaluate fatigue with the recommended complete blood count and metabolic assessment, with consideration of anemia of chronic kidney disease if confirmed. Management options, including iron assessment and erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy, should be determined by nephrology based on hemoglobin and iron results; no such therapy is presumed to be in use.

Provide counseling regarding sodium restriction, avoidance of excessive fluid intake unless otherwise directed, moderation of dietary phosphorus and potassium based on laboratory results, adequate but not excessive protein intake, and avoidance of unregulated supplements. A renal dietitian may be beneficial.

Strongly recommend smoking cessation and offer cessation support. Continue aggressive cardiovascular risk reduction, including review of lipid management and secondary prevention for coronary artery disease once current medications and tolerance are confirmed.

Discuss warning symptoms requiring urgent evaluation, including new or worsening shortness of breath, chest pain, progressive swelling, markedly reduced urine output, persistent vomiting, confusion, severe weakness, or new tremor or unsteadiness.

Arrange close follow-up after laboratory evaluation and nephrology assessment, preferably within a short interval given the eGFR of 9 mL/min/1.73 m² and elevated BUN.
