Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for hypertension and review of blood pressure control.

Subjective

Patrick Harper is a 72-year-old man presenting for routine outpatient follow-up of hypertension, present for approximately 36 months and currently without an established management regimen. He reports intermittent dizziness. He denies headache, blurred vision, chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, fatigue, and epistaxis. No symptoms suggestive of an acute hypertensive emergency are reported today.

He has a 15-year history of coronary artery disease, which is clinically relevant to blood pressure risk reduction. He does not smoke. Current height is 187 cm, weight 86.4 kg, and BMI 24.7 kg/m².

Objective

Vital Signs: Blood pressure 152/93 mmHg. Pulse 74 bpm. Respiratory rate 18/min. Temperature 36.6°C. Oxygen saturation 98% on room air.

Laboratory Data: Serum sodium 142 mmol/L. Serum potassium 3.6 mmol/L.

Physical Examination: Blood pressure is elevated on today’s measurement. Cardiopulmonary status is clinically stable by vital signs, with no reported chest pain, dyspnea, or palpitations. No additional abnormal examination findings are documented in the information available for this visit.

Assessment

Essential hypertension with persistently elevated office blood pressure and intermittent dizziness. Hypertension has been present for approximately three years and is not currently being actively managed. There are no reported findings of hypertensive crisis or acute end-organ symptoms today. Given his age and established coronary artery disease, sustained blood pressure elevation warrants prompt outpatient treatment and confirmation with repeat measurements while avoiding excessive lowering that could worsen dizziness or coronary perfusion.

Coronary artery disease, longstanding by history, without current angina or heart failure symptoms reported today. This increases the importance of cardiovascular risk-factor management.

Serum electrolytes are acceptable for outpatient management, with sodium 142 mmol/L and potassium at the low end of the normal range at 3.6 mmol/L.

Plan

Confirm the blood pressure pattern with repeat office measurements and home blood pressure monitoring, including readings at different times of day and documentation of associated dizziness. Orthostatic blood pressure and pulse measurements are appropriate given his dizziness and age.

Initiate a structured antihypertensive treatment discussion, including the benefits and risks of an ACE inhibitor or ARB, calcium-channel blocker, or thiazide-type diuretic. Medication selection should account for his coronary artery disease, baseline potassium of 3.6 mmol/L, dizziness, renal function, allergies, and any medications identified during formal medication reconciliation. He should not self-start or adjust therapy without clinician guidance.

Recommend a low-sodium eating pattern, regular physical activity as tolerated, moderation of alcohol if applicable, and continued avoidance of tobacco. His BMI is currently 24.7 kg/m², so weight loss is not the primary intervention.

Obtain baseline renal function and repeat electrolytes if pharmacologic therapy is started, particularly after initiation or dose adjustment of an ACE inhibitor, ARB, or diuretic. Recommend ECG, lipid profile, and urinalysis as part of cardiovascular risk and hypertension assessment if not recently completed.

Continue monitoring for symptoms related to coronary artery disease. Seek urgent medical attention for chest pain, new shortness of breath, focal neurologic symptoms, severe headache, acute visual change, or markedly elevated blood pressure accompanied by symptoms.

Arrange close outpatient follow-up after home readings are obtained and after treatment initiation or adjustment to assess blood pressure response, dizziness, renal function, and electrolytes.
