Assessment

1. Chronic kidney disease with markedly reduced renal function

Longstanding chronic kidney disease in an 80-year-old man with a 31-year history of disease and 33-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus. Current creatinine of 5.98 mg/dL, BUN 73 mg/dL, and estimated GFR 8 mL/min/1.73 m² indicate severe renal impairment. The condition is not currently under active management, placing him at high risk for uremic complications, electrolyte abnormalities, acid-base disturbances, anemia, mineral and bone complications, and fluid overload.

2. Type 2 diabetes mellitus, longstanding

Thirty-three-year history of diabetes and likely major contributor to chronic kidney disease. Current glycemic control cannot be assessed from the available information. Any diabetes treatment will require careful consideration of renal function and hypoglycemia risk.

3. Elevated blood pressure reading

Blood pressure is 143/91 mmHg today. Blood pressure control is particularly important in the setting of advanced chronic kidney disease. No current antihypertensive regimen is provided.

Plan

Chronic kidney disease: This degree of renal impairment requires prompt nephrology evaluation rather than routine observation. Recommend urgent assessment by nephrology for comprehensive management and discussion of renal replacement therapy options, including dialysis planning when clinically appropriate, as well as goals of care and treatment preferences. Obtain updated renal evaluation with serum electrolytes, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphorus, CBC, intact parathyroid hormone, urinalysis, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and repeat renal function testing. Consider renal ultrasound if not previously performed or if clinically indicated.

Review for complications of advanced kidney disease, including anemia, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, mineral and bone disorder, worsening uremic symptoms, and volume overload. Medication reconciliation is essential, with avoidance of nephrotoxic agents and renal dosing of all medications. Do not use nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs unless specifically directed by a treating clinician.

The patient should seek emergency care for new or worsening shortness of breath, chest pain, confusion, marked weakness, persistent nausea or vomiting, inability to maintain oral intake, rapidly increasing swelling, substantially reduced urine output, or other acute deterioration.

Diabetes mellitus: Review home glucose monitoring, current diabetes medications, hypoglycemia history, and diabetic complications at the next visit. Glycemic management should be individualized for age and advanced renal impairment, with avoidance of hypoglycemia. Coordinate diabetes care with renal management.

Blood pressure: Repeat blood pressure measurement and obtain home readings if feasible. Review sodium intake and current medications before selecting or adjusting therapy. Blood pressure management should be coordinated with nephrology given the degree of renal dysfunction.

Follow-up: Arrange prompt follow-up after nephrology evaluation and updated laboratory testing. This patient should not be managed with routine long-interval follow-up given the severity of renal impairment.
