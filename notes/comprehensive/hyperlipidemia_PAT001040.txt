Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for untreated hyperlipidemia.

Subjective

Brian Simmons is a 47-year-old man presenting for routine outpatient follow-up and review of significant dyslipidemia. Hyperlipidemia has been present for approximately 7 years and is currently not being treated. Recent lipid results show total cholesterol 325 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol 216 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol 34 mg/dL, and triglycerides 377 mg/dL.

He denies chest discomfort, exertional leg pain or intermittent claudication, dizziness, xanthomas, and xanthelasma. No lipid-lowering medication use is provided. He is an active tobacco smoker. Relevant comorbidities include hypertension for approximately 12 years and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease for approximately 18 years. He has a normal body mass index and weighs 75.5 kg at a height of 182 cm.

Assessment

Marked untreated hyperlipidemia with substantially elevated LDL cholesterol, elevated triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, and elevated total cholesterol. The LDL of 216 mg/dL represents a significant atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk requiring active management, even in the absence of current chest discomfort or claudication. The lipid pattern may reflect a combination of genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors; secondary contributors should also be assessed.

Tobacco use, which further increases cardiovascular risk.

Hypertension, present for approximately 12 years, with current blood pressure 134/87 mmHg.

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, present for approximately 18 years. This is not a contraindication to statin therapy, although hepatic status should be reviewed as part of treatment planning.

Plan

Discussed the markedly abnormal lipid profile and the importance of initiating pharmacologic therapy rather than continuing observation alone. Recommend starting a high-intensity statin, such as atorvastatin or rosuvastatin, after confirming patient agreement and reviewing baseline hepatic status, medication interactions, and any prior intolerance. Given the LDL cholesterol above 190 mg/dL, treatment is indicated independent of calculated short-term risk.

Recommend a fasting lipid panel after initiation of therapy to assess response and adherence, with follow-up generally within 4–12 weeks. If LDL reduction is inadequate despite adherence and maximally tolerated statin therapy, consider addition of ezetimibe and further risk-based evaluation.

Recommend assessment for secondary contributors to dyslipidemia, including thyroid, renal, hepatic, glycemic, and medication-related factors. Consider ApoB and lipoprotein(a) measurement for additional risk characterization. Given the degree of LDL elevation, obtain a more detailed family history of premature cardiovascular disease and consider evaluation for an inherited lipid disorder. Coronary calcium scanning may be considered only if treatment decision-making remains uncertain; it should not delay therapy in the setting of this LDL level.

Triglycerides are elevated at 377 mg/dL. Counsel regarding reduction of refined carbohydrates, added sugars, and alcohol if applicable, while emphasizing dietary patterns low in saturated and trans fats. Encourage regular aerobic and resistance exercise as tolerated. His BMI is 22.8 kg/m², so weight loss is not a primary goal, but maintenance of a healthy weight is encouraged.

Strongly counsel smoking cessation and offer assistance with a quit plan and cessation pharmacotherapy at a subsequent visit if desired.

Continue monitoring blood pressure and reinforce a heart-healthy, low-sodium dietary pattern. No change to antihypertensive therapy is documented today.

Discussed that new chest discomfort, exertional leg pain, unexplained dizziness, or other concerning cardiovascular symptoms should prompt timely medical evaluation.

Return for medication and lipid response review within 4–12 weeks, or sooner for adverse effects or new symptoms.
