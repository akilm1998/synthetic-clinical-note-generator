Assessment

1. Chronic kidney disease with markedly reduced renal filtration

Longstanding chronic kidney disease is reported over approximately 14 years, with current creatinine 2.77 mg/dL, BUN 81 mg/dL, and eGFR 20 mL/min/1.73 m². The elevated BUN and creatinine are consistent with substantially reduced kidney function. He currently reports fatigue but no nausea, pruritus, muscle cramps, sleep disturbance, edema, urinary frequency changes, or dyspnea to suggest clinically prominent uremic or fluid-overload symptoms at this visit. The etiology has not been established from the available history. Longstanding hypertension is a potential contributing factor, although the renal cause should not be presumed without further evaluation.

2. Hypertension

Blood pressure is elevated at 156/91 mmHg in the setting of significant chronic kidney disease. Inadequate blood-pressure control may accelerate further loss of renal function and increase cardiovascular risk. A confirmed home or ambulatory blood-pressure profile and medication reconciliation are important before selecting or adjusting therapy.

3. Fatigue with known anemia of chronic disease

Fatigue may be related to the reported anemia of chronic disease and/or reduced kidney function. Further characterization is appropriate before considering disease-specific treatment. No hemoglobin value is available in the current laboratory data.

4. Hyperlipidemia

Hyperlipidemia has been present for approximately 2 years and adds to his cardiovascular risk in the context of hypertension and chronic kidney disease. The available information does not include a lipid panel or current lipid-lowering treatment.

5. Cardiovascular and renal risk associated with overweight status

BMI is 25.9 kg/m². He does not smoke. Cardiovascular risk reduction, dietary counseling, and avoidance of additional renal insults are important components of care.

Plan

1. Chronic kidney disease

Arrange comprehensive renal evaluation, including repeat renal function testing with serum electrolytes, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphorus, complete blood count, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and intact parathyroid hormone. These studies will help assess proteinuria and common complications of advanced chronic kidney disease, including anemia and mineral-bone disorder.

Obtain renal ultrasound to assess kidney size, echogenicity, and structural or obstructive abnormalities. Additional etiologic evaluation will be guided by the urine findings, imaging, prior records, and clinical course.

Request prior primary-care and hospital records, including historical creatinine values, urinalyses, imaging, and previous renal evaluations, to determine the trajectory of kidney function over the reported 14-year duration.

Avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and other potentially nephrotoxic over-the-counter products. Any new prescription medication should be reviewed for renal dosing. Maintain appropriate hydration and avoid dehydration, particularly during acute gastrointestinal illness.

Discuss a kidney-conscious diet emphasizing moderation of sodium intake and avoidance of excessive protein, with individualized dietary counseling after urine and electrolyte assessment. No empiric phosphate binder, erythropoiesis-stimulating agent, or other CKD-specific medication is initiated from the information currently available.

Review warning symptoms requiring urgent assessment, including new or worsening shortness of breath, rapidly progressive edema, persistent vomiting, confusion, severe weakness, chest pain, or markedly reduced urine output.

2. Hypertension

Confirm blood-pressure control with home readings using a validated upper-arm cuff, including seated measurements at consistent times. Review dietary sodium intake and medication history. Initiate or adjust antihypertensive therapy after medication reconciliation and review of potassium, bicarbonate, urine albumin excretion, and prior tolerance. Renin-angiotensin system blockade may be considered when clinically appropriate, particularly if albuminuria is present, with appropriate renal function and potassium monitoring.

3. Anemia of chronic disease and fatigue

Obtain CBC and iron indices, including ferritin and transferrin saturation, to characterize the anemia and assess for potentially correctable iron deficiency. Additional evaluation will depend on those results. Treatment with iron or an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent should not be started without reviewing hemoglobin, iron status, symptoms, and risks.

4. Hyperlipidemia

Obtain an updated lipid profile and review prior cardiovascular history and treatment records. Counsel regarding a heart-healthy, kidney-conscious diet and regular physical activity as tolerated. Lipid-lowering therapy should be determined after medication reconciliation and overall cardiovascular risk review.

5. Follow-up and renal replacement planning

Close nephrology follow-up is recommended given the eGFR of 20 mL/min/1.73 m², BUN of 81 mg/dL, longstanding disease, and currently elevated blood pressure. At follow-up, review the renal trend, urine albumin, electrolyte profile, anemia assessment, mineral-bone parameters, renal ultrasound, and blood-pressure log. Education regarding future kidney replacement options should be introduced progressively if renal function continues to decline, while recognizing that he currently has no reported dyspnea, edema, nausea, confusion, or other symptoms indicating an immediate need for dialysis based on today’s history.
