Assessment

Chronic kidney disease with markedly reduced renal function and significant azotemia, present for approximately 31 years. Current creatinine is 4.21 mg/dL, BUN 89 mg/dL, and estimated GFR 15 mL/min/1.73 m². He reports fatigue but no nausea, pruritus, muscle cramps, sleep disturbance, urinary change, edema, or dyspnea to suggest clinically apparent uremic or fluid-overload symptoms at this visit. The condition is not currently documented as being under active medication management, and the medication review cannot establish effectiveness or adherence to a specific regimen.

Longstanding type 2 diabetes mellitus and hyperlipidemia are important comorbidities. Anemia of chronic disease may contribute to fatigue. Blood pressure is mildly elevated at 143/85 mmHg, although a diagnosis of hypertension is not established from the information provided.

Plan

Medication review and reconciliation: Confirm the complete current medication list, including prescription medications, over-the-counter agents, supplements, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, and any renal-directed therapies. Review renal dosing and avoidance of nephrotoxic medications. No new medication is initiated today without a verified medication list and current renal safety review.

Renal disease management: Recommend prompt nephrology involvement for comprehensive management of advanced chronic kidney disease, assessment of progression, medication optimization, and discussion of future renal replacement planning as clinically appropriate. This recommendation is based on the substantially reduced estimated GFR and BUN of 89 mg/dL, despite the absence of acute fluid-overload or prominent uremic symptoms today.

Recommended monitoring includes repeat renal function and serum electrolytes, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, complete blood count to evaluate the contribution of anemia to fatigue, and intact parathyroid hormone assessment for chronic kidney disease mineral-bone complications. Renal ultrasound may be considered if not previously characterized or if clinically indicated.

Diabetes and cardiovascular risk: Continue close glycemic and lipid management with renal-appropriate medication selection and dosing. Review blood pressure trends rather than relying on a single reading; reinforce sodium moderation and avoidance of dehydration. Medication choices should account for the current renal function.

Anemia: Evaluate current blood counts and iron status as clinically appropriate before considering or adjusting anemia-directed therapy. Fatigue may be multifactorial, with chronic kidney disease and anemia of chronic disease both potential contributors.

Education: Discussed the importance of medication reconciliation, adherence to any verified renal and diabetic regimen, avoidance of NSAIDs unless specifically directed, and prompt evaluation for worsening shortness of breath, new swelling, markedly reduced urine output, persistent nausea or vomiting, confusion, severe weakness, or chest discomfort.

Follow-up: Arrange close primary care follow-up after medication reconciliation and laboratory monitoring, with nephrology follow-up recommended promptly.
