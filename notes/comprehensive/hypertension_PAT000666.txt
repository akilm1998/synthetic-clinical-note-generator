Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for longstanding hypertension.

Subjective

Kevin Peterson is an 84-year-old man presenting for routine outpatient follow-up of essential hypertension of approximately 20 years’ duration. His hypertension is currently not being actively managed. He reports no headache, dizziness, blurred vision, chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, fatigue, or epistaxis. No interval hypertensive symptoms are reported.

Relevant comorbidities include coronary artery disease of approximately 38 years’ duration and type 2 diabetes mellitus of approximately 29 years’ duration. He continues to smoke. Weight is stable by the available measurements, with BMI 23.3 kg/m². No medication regimen or medication adherence history is provided.

Review of systems is negative for the documented hypertension-associated symptoms, including neurologic symptoms, visual disturbance, cardiopulmonary complaints, palpitations, fatigue, and nosebleeds.

Objective

Vital Signs: Blood pressure 149/88 mmHg. Pulse 83 bpm. Respiratory rate 16/min. Temperature 37.0°C. Oxygen saturation 98% on room air.

Height 181 cm. Weight 76.3 kg. BMI 23.3 kg/m².

Laboratory data: Serum sodium 142 mmol/L. Serum potassium 3.9 mmol/L.

Physical examination: Cardiopulmonary and neurologic examination findings are not otherwise documented in the available encounter data. The patient has no reported symptoms suggestive of acute hypertensive end-organ injury.

Assessment

Essential hypertension, longstanding, with current office blood pressure of 149/88 mmHg. Although he is asymptomatic and there is no reported evidence of acute hypertensive crisis, blood pressure remains above goal for an older adult with established coronary artery disease and diabetes mellitus. Given the prolonged duration of hypertension and the absence of active management, cardiovascular and renal risk reduction require renewed attention.

Coronary artery disease, longstanding.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus, longstanding.

Active tobacco use, contributing to cardiovascular risk.

Serum sodium and potassium are within expected physiologic ranges on the available testing.

Plan

Discussed the importance of active blood pressure management in the setting of advanced age, longstanding hypertension, coronary artery disease, and diabetes. Recommend confirmation of his out-of-office blood pressure pattern with properly performed home measurements, including seated readings after several minutes of rest, and review of a written blood pressure log at follow-up.

Recommend initiation or reestablishment of antihypertensive therapy through shared decision-making, with selection guided by his coronary artery disease, diabetes, renal status, orthostatic tolerance, and medication history. Medication reconciliation and assessment of adherence should be completed before finalizing the regimen. Avoid overly rapid reduction in blood pressure given his age and vascular comorbidity.

Recommend assessment of renal function and urinary protein, repeat serum electrolytes after any treatment initiation or dose adjustment, urinalysis, lipid profile, and electrocardiogram as part of cardiovascular risk and hypertensive end-organ evaluation. The available sodium and potassium values are acceptable for consideration of standard antihypertensive therapy, but renal function is needed for complete treatment selection and monitoring.

Counseled regarding a lower-sodium dietary pattern, avoidance of excess alcohol if applicable, regular physical activity as tolerated, and maintenance of his healthy body weight. Strongly advised smoking cessation and offered structured cessation support.

Reviewed symptoms requiring urgent evaluation, including severe or sudden headache, new neurologic deficit, acute visual change, chest pain, or shortness of breath.

Follow up after home blood pressure monitoring and medication reconciliation for reassessment of control and adjustment of therapy.
