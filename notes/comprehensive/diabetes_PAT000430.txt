Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Subjective

Damian Boyd is an 87-year-old man presenting for outpatient follow-up of type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosed approximately 24 years ago. Current disease control is poor, with no active diabetes-management regimen provided at this visit. He denies increased thirst, frequent urination, increased hunger, unintentional weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, or recurrent infections. He reports tingling in the hands or feet. No numbness is reported.

He has a history of anxiety for approximately 56 years and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease for approximately 44 years. He does not smoke. Weight is 66.9 kg at a height of 176 cm, with BMI 21.6 kg/m².

Review of Systems

Constitutional: Denies fatigue and unintentional weight loss.

Eyes: Denies blurred vision.

Endocrine: Denies increased thirst, frequent urination, and increased hunger.

Skin: Denies slow-healing wounds.

Infectious: Denies frequent infections.

Neurologic: Reports tingling in the hands or feet. Denies numbness in the hands or feet.

Psychiatric: History of anxiety.

Objective

Vital Signs: Blood pressure 133/77 mmHg, pulse 74 beats/minute, respiratory rate 19/minute, temperature 36.8°C, oxygen saturation 96%.

Height 176 cm. Weight 66.9 kg. BMI 21.6 kg/m².

Laboratory Data: Hemoglobin A1c 9.9%. Fasting glucose 247 mg/dL.

Physical Examination

General: Elderly male presenting for routine ambulatory follow-up; BMI is within the normal range.

Cardiovascular: Pulse 74 beats/minute and blood pressure 133/77 mmHg.

Respiratory: Respiratory rate 19/minute with oxygen saturation 96% on the recorded vital-sign assessment.

Neurologic: Reports distal tingling involving the hands or feet; no numbness reported.

The remainder of the examination is directed toward assessment of diabetes, including skin, peripheral circulation, and foot sensation.

Assessment

1. Type 2 diabetes mellitus without documented complication, currently inadequately controlled.

Hemoglobin A1c of 9.9% and fasting glucose of 247 mg/dL indicate substantial hyperglycemia despite the absence of classic osmotic or constitutional symptoms. The patient has had diabetes for approximately 24 years and is not currently described as having an active management regimen. His normal BMI and advanced age do not lessen the significance of the current glycemic elevation. Distal tingling raises concern for possible early peripheral nerve involvement, although a diabetes-related neurologic complication is not established from the information available. There are no reported wounds, recurrent infections, visual symptoms, or unintentional weight loss.

2. Distal paresthesia.

Tingling in the hands or feet warrants focused sensory and foot examination, with attention to protective sensation, skin integrity, pulses, and fall risk. Further evaluation should be guided by the examination and clinical course.

3. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.

Longstanding history, approximately 44 years. Glycemic control is clinically relevant to ongoing metabolic liver disease management. No additional liver laboratory values are provided today.

4. Anxiety.

Longstanding history, approximately 56 years. No acute psychiatric symptoms are reported in the current review.

Plan

1. Diabetes mellitus

Discussed that the current HbA1c and fasting glucose reflect inadequate glycemic control and increased risk for future microvascular and macrovascular complications. Review dietary pattern, carbohydrate intake, meal regularity, physical activity appropriate for age and functional status, home glucose monitoring, and barriers to treatment adherence.

Given his age and the degree of hyperglycemia, treatment should be individualized with emphasis on meaningful glucose reduction while avoiding hypoglycemia, dehydration, weight loss, and treatment burden. A medication regimen should be reviewed and initiated or adjusted through shared decision-making; potential options include metformin when renal and hepatic status are appropriate, or other glucose-lowering therapy selected according to kidney function, liver status, nutritional intake, functional status, and hypoglycemia risk. Insulin or a sulfonylurea would require particular caution in this older adult because of hypoglycemia risk. No current medication regimen is documented, so no medication changes are recorded today.

Recommend assessment of renal function with serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and a fasting lipid profile to guide comprehensive diabetes risk reduction. Repeat HbA1c after an appropriate interval following treatment implementation. Consider random glucose assessment if clinically indicated.

Perform and document a complete diabetic foot examination, including skin inspection, pulses, and protective sensation. Counsel on daily foot inspection, appropriate footwear, and prompt evaluation of any skin breakdown or wound. Recommend a dilated eye examination because of the long duration of diabetes and current hyperglycemia.

2. Distal tingling

Evaluate the distribution and severity of paresthesias, gait stability, and protective sensation. Review for reversible contributors as clinically appropriate. Reinforce fall-prevention measures and foot care. Prompt evaluation is advised for new weakness, progressive sensory loss, balance difficulty, or development of a foot lesion.

3. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

Continue metabolic risk-factor management through improved glycemic control, avoidance of excess alcohol, and review of nutrition and physical activity. Consider monitoring liver enzymes and reassessing hepatic risk as clinically appropriate; no additional liver results are available for interpretation today.

4. Anxiety

Continue to assess anxiety symptoms, sleep, mood, functional impact, and safety at follow-up. Coordinate treatment decisions with the patient’s preferences and overall medical goals.

5. Follow-up

Arrange close outpatient follow-up to review glucose readings, treatment tolerance, hypoglycemia symptoms, foot findings, and repeat glycemic assessment. Earlier evaluation is appropriate for marked hyperglycemia, vomiting, dehydration, confusion, new visual changes, or any nonhealing wound.
