Chief Complaint

Routine follow-up for type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Subjective

Alexandra Boyd is a 98-year-old woman presenting for routine outpatient follow-up of type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosed approximately 25 years ago. Current glycemic control is poor, with hemoglobin A1c 10.0% and fasting glucose 246 mg/dL. She reports increased thirst, frequent urination, increased hunger, unintentional weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, and frequent infections are all absent. She does report numbness and tingling involving the hands and feet.

She has longstanding obesity, present for approximately 74 years, with current weight 115.5 kg at a height of 173 cm, BMI 38.6 kg/m². Hypertension has been present for approximately 2 years. She also has a longstanding history of depression, present for approximately 34 years. No smoking history.

Constitutional: Denies fatigue and unintentional weight loss.

Eyes: Denies blurred vision.

Neurologic: Reports numbness and tingling of the hands and feet.

Infectious: Denies frequent infections.

Objective

Vital Signs: Blood pressure 145/88 mmHg, pulse 79 bpm, respiratory rate 20/min, temperature 37.0°C, oxygen saturation 97%.

Height 173 cm. Weight 115.5 kg. BMI 38.6 kg/m².

General: Elderly woman seen for outpatient follow-up. Obesity noted. No acute distress described.

Laboratory Data: Hemoglobin A1c 10.0%. Fasting glucose 246 mg/dL.

Assessment

1. Type 2 diabetes mellitus without documented complications, with markedly elevated fasting glucose and hemoglobin A1c. Glycemic control is substantially above goal and requires active reassessment. She denies classic hyperglycemic symptoms but reports distal numbness and tingling of the hands and feet, which may represent diabetic peripheral nerve symptoms and warrants focused examination and further evaluation. Given her advanced age, treatment should be individualized with attention to avoiding hypoglycemia, maintaining function, and considering overall goals of care.

2. Paresthesias of the hands and feet. Symptoms are concerning for possible peripheral neuropathy in the setting of long-duration diabetes. No wounds or recurrent infections are reported.

3. Obesity, BMI 38.6 kg/m², longstanding.

4. Hypertension with blood pressure 145/88 mmHg today. Control is above usual outpatient targets, though treatment decisions should account for age, orthostasis risk, renal status, and overall clinical priorities.

5. Depression, longstanding. No acute psychiatric concern reported today.

Plan

1. Diabetes: Review the current diabetes treatment approach and barriers to care in detail. Confirm whether she has an established medication regimen, ability to obtain and administer medications, dietary pattern, glucose-monitoring capability, and availability of caregiver support before initiating or intensifying therapy. Discuss treatment options appropriate for a 98-year-old patient with a focus on simple regimens, prevention of symptomatic hyperglycemia, and minimizing hypoglycemia. Avoid rapid or overly aggressive glucose lowering.

2. Encourage a practical nutrition plan emphasizing consistent carbohydrate intake, reduced concentrated sugars, adequate hydration, and portion control. Recommend activity appropriate to her mobility and fall risk, such as gentle walking or chair-based exercise as tolerated.

3. Obtain or review renal function, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and fasting lipid profile before finalizing medication choices and cardiovascular risk management. Repeat hemoglobin A1c after an appropriate interval to assess response to treatment.

4. Perform a complete diabetic foot examination at follow-up, including skin inspection, pulses, and sensation. Counsel her to inspect her feet regularly, avoid barefoot walking, and report any skin breakdown, redness, drainage, or nonhealing area promptly. Consider evaluation of other reversible contributors to paresthesias if clinically indicated.

5. Hypertension: Recheck blood pressure with attention to proper technique and symptoms of dizziness or orthostasis. Review home readings if available. Consider treatment adjustment based on repeated measurements, renal function, fall risk, and individualized goals.

6. Obesity: Continue counseling regarding sustainable dietary changes and safe physical activity. Weight reduction may improve glycemic and blood pressure control, but recommendations should remain realistic and avoid loss of strength or nutritional compromise.

7. Depression: Continue to assess mood, sleep, appetite, energy, cognition, and safety at subsequent visits. Coordinate management with her established supports if concerns emerge.

8. Follow up promptly to review the diabetes treatment plan and glucose monitoring. Seek urgent care for persistent vomiting, inability to maintain hydration, confusion, marked weakness, severe drowsiness, or persistently very high glucose readings.
