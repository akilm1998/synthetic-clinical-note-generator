Assessment

Chronic kidney disease with longstanding duration of approximately 24 years and markedly reduced estimated GFR. Creatinine and BUN are substantially elevated, consistent with significant chronic renal impairment. He currently reports fatigue but no nausea, pruritus, muscle cramps, sleep disturbance, change in urination, edema, or dyspnea to suggest symptomatic uremia or clinically apparent fluid overload at this visit.

Anemia of chronic disease, longstanding for approximately 18 years, likely contributing to fatigue; current blood counts are not available in the supplied data.

Secondary hyperparathyroidism, longstanding for approximately 23 years, requiring reassessment of mineral-bone parameters.

Blood pressure is elevated at 148/86 mmHg. A single office reading is insufficient to characterize overall control, but hypertension is clinically relevant in the setting of chronic kidney disease.

Overweight by BMI at 28.6 kg/m².

Plan

Discussed the significance of the current renal indices and the need for active renal disease management despite the absence of acute uremic or volume-overload symptoms.

Recommend prompt nephrology evaluation for comprehensive management, assessment of renal trajectory, review of indications for renal replacement planning, and management of chronic kidney disease complications.

Recommend repeat renal function assessment with serum creatinine, BUN, estimated GFR, and serum electrolytes. Obtain urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio to further characterize kidney disease. Recommend CBC for evaluation of longstanding anemia and intact parathyroid hormone assessment in the setting of secondary hyperparathyroidism. Renal ultrasound may be considered as part of structural assessment if not previously completed.

Review all prescription and over-the-counter medications at the next visit for renal dosing and avoidance of nephrotoxic agents, particularly NSAIDs. Encourage avoidance of dehydration and prompt evaluation for vomiting, worsening weakness, reduced urine output, new swelling, shortness of breath, confusion, or persistent nausea.

Continue outpatient blood pressure monitoring and reassess management after reviewing serial readings and the complete medication list. Discussed that blood pressure control is important to reduce further renal injury.

Follow up promptly after recommended renal and complication-focused evaluation, or sooner for any new uremic or fluid-overload symptoms.
